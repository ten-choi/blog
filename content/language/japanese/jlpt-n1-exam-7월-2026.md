On Sunday, July 5, 2026, I took my second JLPT — and this time it was the **N1**, at a test site in Fukuoka, Japan.

## 🏫 The Test Site: 九州産業大学

My assigned test site was **Kyushu Sangyo University (九州産業大学)**. 
I'd actually been there before for a TOEIC exam, so finding the venue was no trouble at all.

## 🎒 What to Bring

Make sure you've got these with you on exam day:

- **Your test voucher** — no voucher, no exam. Double-check for it the night before.
- **Pencils and an eraser**
- **A watch** — there was no clock in my test room, both for this JLPT and for the TOEIC I took at the same university. It probably depends on the room, but don't count on it — bring your own.

## 📚 Why I Took the N1

I passed the N2 back in 2019, and now, in 2026, I decided to try the N1 in Japan. To be honest, I didn't study that hard for it — I just wanted to sit the exam. I *do* study Japanese consistently, but what I really wanted was that sense of pressure you only get from a real test.

Day-to-day conversation with native speakers doesn't bother me at all, but the N1 demands a huge amount of kanji, and that's easily my weakest point. My approach has always been *"learn a little at a time, over a long time"* — after all, I live and work in Japan, so there's no rush.

As for the exam itself: **listening and grammar** gave me no trouble whatsoever. The kanji I didn't know? Well, there was nothing I could do about those on the spot.

---

## 🌧️ A Lesson About Fukuoka Weather

Here's something I learned the hard way this time:

> ⚠️ **If you're taking the JLPT in Fukuoka, plan for weather-related transit delays.**

I made it to Hakata Station just fine, but the subway line toward the test venue was delayed by about **an hour** because of the weather. After weighing my options, I ended up taking a taxi — and as you probably know, taxis in Japan are *not* cheap.

It worked out for me this time, but imagine someone who spent months preparing getting hit with that kind of delay right before the exam — it could really shake you mentally. If you're taking the test in Fukuoka, build in a generous buffer for transit.

---

## ✍️ Final Thoughts

The exam felt just like I remembered: grammar and listening were genuinely easy for me, thanks to living in Japan for about three years. Since I hadn't seriously prepared for the kanji, I'm not expecting much from my result.

But I got something valuable out of it anyway: the experience of taking an exam in Japan. Sure, the process is basically the same as in Korea — but it's still a test in a foreign country, and I wanted to experience it firsthand. Trying something out with your own two feet before it really counts is, in my experience, one of the most useful things you can do.
