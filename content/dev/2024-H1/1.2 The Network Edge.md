# 1.2 The Network Edge

Let's take a closer look at the components of a computer network (the Internet in particular).

<br/>

### Hosts and End Systems

- **Computers and other devices connected to the Internet**
- They're called **"end" systems** because they sit at the edge of the Internet.
- End systems are also called **hosts** because they run (host) applications. Hosts are divided into `clients` and `servers`.

## 1.2.1 Access Networks

### Access Network

The **network that connects an end system to the first router** (the **edge router**) on the path from that end system to any other distant end system.

The thick lines in the figure below show several kinds of access networks.

<p align="center"><img width="450" alt="Access networks" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210129140-31bb75d0-c8f9-4a70-bb54-60c805b1fea8.png">

<br/>


Let's go through the network access technologies one by one.

- `Home access`: DSL, cable, FTTH, 5G fixed wireless
- `Enterprise (and home) access`: Ethernet, WiFi
- `Wide-area wireless access`: 3G, LTE 4G, 5G

<br/>

## Home Access

- The most widespread types of broadband home access today (2020)
    - `DSL`: uses the local telephone company's (telco's) **existing local telephone infrastructure**
    - `Cable`: uses the cable TV company's **existing cable TV infrastructure**


- `FTTH`: a technology of the future that offers higher speeds than the access types above

<br/>

### DSL (Digital Subscriber Line) Internet Access

A home gets `DSL Internet access` **from the same local telephone company (telco)** that provides its wired local phone service.

<br/>

#### DSL Modem

<p align="center"><img width="550" alt="DSL Internet access" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210128274-3b65a140-9c16-4b41-8f6d-5da5099f1a68.png">
<br/>
<br/>


The home's DSL modem uses the existing telephone line **to exchange data with a DSLAM (Digital Subscriber Line Access Multiplexer)**  
located in the telco's local central office (CO).

1. The home DSL modem takes digital data and converts it into high-frequency tones for transmission over the telephone line to the CO.
2. The analog signals from many homes are **converted back into digital format at the DSLAM.**

<br/>

#### Frequency-Division Multiplexing (FDM)

_This is covered in detail in Section 1.3.2._

> The home telephone line **carries both data and traditional telephone signals at the same time**, encoded at different frequencies.

<br/>

Thanks to frequency-division multiplexing, a single DSL link looks like three separate links.

This lets **a phone call and an Internet connection share the DSL link at the same time.**

- High-speed downstream channel: 50 kHz to 1 MHz band
- Medium-speed upstream channel: 40 to 50 kHz band
- Ordinary two-way telephone channel: 0 to 4 kHz band

<br/>

#### Splitter

- Sits on the home side.
- Role
    1. Separates the data and telephone signals arriving at the home
    2. Forwards the data signal to the DSL modem

<br/>

#### DSLAM (Digital Subscriber Line Access Multiplexer)

- Hundreds to thousands of homes connect to a DSLAM.
- Role
    1. Separates the data and phone signals
    2. Sends the data into the Internet

<br/>

#### DSL Standards

- DSL standards define multiple `transmission rates`, covering both upstream and downstream. **(The downstream channel is allocated a higher rate than the upstream channel.)**
    - `Upstream` rates: 3.5 Mbps, 16 Mbps
    - `Downstream` rates: 24 Mbps, 52 Mbps

<br/>

- Latest standard: defines aggregate upstream plus downstream rates of 1 Gbps (ITU 2014)
    - Because the downstream and upstream rates differ, this kind of access is called <b>asymmetric</b>.

<br/>

### Cable Internet Access, HFC (Hybrid Fiber Coax)

A home gets Internet access **from the same company that provides its cable TV service**.

Fiber optics connect the `cable head end` to neighborhood-level junctions, and from there traditional `coaxial cable` is used to reach individual homes.

<p align="center"><img width="650" alt="HFC access network" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210128378-c0754ede-9bca-4e37-9863-b3fb127a6679.png">
<br/>
<br/>

#### Head End

- The (cable TV) signal relay station / main transmission facility (distribution center)
- A facility that **processes, amplifies, and then distributes the signals** received from each source so that many households can watch them
- Refers to all the equipment that amplifies, adjusts, converts, blocks, or mixes signals for cable TV broadcasting and sends them out over the trunk cables.

<br/>

#### Cable Modem

- A modem for cable Internet access
- Connects to the home PC through an `Ethernet port`.
- At the cable head end sits the `CMTS (Cable Modem Termination System)`.
- The cable modem divides the HFC network into two channels, downstream and upstream. _(Same as DSL!)_
    - Asymmetric access
    - The downstream channel is allocated a higher rate than the upstream channel.

<br/>

#### CMTS (Cable Modem Termination System)

- **Converts the analog signals sent from the cable modems** in many downstream homes **back into digital format**
- In other words, it plays a role similar to the DSLAM in a DSL network.

<br/>

#### Cable Internet Is a Shared Broadcast Medium ⭐️

1. `Every packet sent by the head end` / travels **down the downstream channel** on every link / to every home.
2. `Every packet sent by a home` / travels **up the upstream channel** / to the head end.

<br/>

This leads to the following situations:

- If several users are simultaneously downloading **different** video files on the downstream channel,  
  the actual rate at which each user receives their file **will be lower than the downstream rate.**
- If only a few users are active and they're all just browsing the web, each user may receive web pages at **the full downstream rate**.

Because the upstream channel is shared too, a `distributed multiple access protocol` is needed to coordinate transmissions and avoid collisions.

<br/>

### FTTH (Fiber to the Home) Internet Access

- **Provides an optical fiber path directly** from the local central office (CO) to the home.
- Can potentially offer Internet access rates in the Gbps range.
- `Optical distribution technologies`: technologies for distributing optical signals from the CO to the homes.

<br/>

#### Direct Fiber

- The simplest optical distribution network
- One fiber runs from the CO to each home

<br/>

#### AON (Active Optical Network) and PON (Passive Optical Network)

These are two competing **optical distribution network architectures** that perform splitting.

`Splitting`: each fiber leaving the CO is usually shared by many homes, so **a single fiber runs until it gets relatively close to the homes and is then split into individual customer-specific fibers**.

- `AON`: essentially switched Ethernet
- `PON`: see the figure below (FTTH Internet access using a PON distribution architecture)

  <p align="center"><img width="650" alt="FTTH Internet access" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210128431-993b57ae-7275-4d65-a81e-2eb519a1c51f.png"><br/><br/>

  As shown, users in each home connect a home router to the ONT and access the Internet through it.<br/><br/>
  The access process can be summarized as follows:
    1. Each home has an `ONT (Optical Network Terminator)`, which is **connected to a neighborhood splitter** by a dedicated optical fiber.
    2. The `splitter (optical splitter)` **combines several homes onto a single shared optical fiber**, which connects to an `OLT (Optical Line Terminator)` in the telco's CO.
    3. The OLT **converts between optical and electrical signals** and connects to the `Internet` via a telco router.

<br/>

### 5G Fixed Wireless (5G-FW)

- Uses `beam-forming technology` to send data **wirelessly** from the provider's base station to a modem in the home.
- Just as a WiFi wireless router connects to a cable or DSL modem, in 5G-FW a WiFi wireless router is connected to the modem.
- `5G cellular networks` _→ covered in Chapter 7_

### Wireless LAN Settings

More and more, people access the Internet **wirelessly** from "things" (smartphones, tablets, and so on).

- In a wireless LAN setting, wireless users send and receive packets to and from an `AP (Access Point)` connected to the enterprise network.
- The AP is in turn connected to the wired network.
- `WiFi`: wireless LAN access based on the IEEE 802.11 technology

## Wide-Area Wireless Access

- **Uses wireless infrastructure** (the same infrastructure mobile carriers use to send and receive packets through the base stations they operate)
- Unlike WiFi, where you need to be within a few tens of meters of the access point, a user only needs to be **within a few tens of kilometers of the base station**.

## 1.2.2 Physical Media

Let's look at the physical media commonly used on the Internet, along with a few others.

<br/>

### Physical Media

To define physical media, we first need to think about bits.

<br/>

```
Consider a single bit traveling from one end system, through a series of links and routers, to another end system.
```

- The bit passes through several routers. (First router: receives & transmits the bit → second router: receives & transmits the bit → third router: …)
- In other words, as a bit travels from source to destination it is transmitted many times, passing through `a series of transmitter-receiver pairs`.

<br/>

> A bit is sent by propagating electromagnetic waves or optical pulses **across a physical medium**.

- Physical media come in many forms and **don't have to be the same type for each transmitter-receiver pair along the path.**
- e.g., twisted-pair copper wire, coaxial cable, multimode fiber-optic cable, terrestrial radio spectrum, and satellite radio spectrum
- Two categories
    - `Guided media`: the waves are **guided along a solid medium** such as twisted-pair copper wire, coaxial cable, or fiber-optic cable
    - `Unguided media`: the waves **propagate through the open air**, as in a wireless LAN or a digital satellite channel

<br/>

### Twisted-Pair Copper Wire

- The cheapest and most commonly used transmission medium (over 99% of the wired connections from telephone handsets to the local telephone switch use it)
- Structure
    - Two insulated copper wires, each about 1 mm thick, arranged in a **regular spiral pattern**.
    - The wires are twisted together to reduce electrical interference from similar pairs nearby, and **one pair of wires makes up a single communication link.**
- Data rate: depends on **the thickness of the wire and the distance between transmitter and receiver**.
- Usage: UTP (Unshielded Twisted Pair) is the most common medium for computer networks within buildings, i.e., LANs.

<br/>

### Coaxial Cable

- Structure: like twisted pair, it consists of two copper conductors, but they're **concentric** rather than parallel.
- Data rate: thanks to its concentric construction and special insulation and shielding, it can achieve **higher data rates than twisted pair**.
- Usage: cable TV systems
- Characteristics
    - Can be used as a `guided shared medium`.
    - Many end systems can connect directly to the cable, and every end system **receives whatever any of the other end systems sends.**

<br/>

### Optical Fiber

- A thin, flexible medium that conducts pulses of light, with each pulse representing a bit
- Supports very high bit rates, from 10 to 100 gigabits per second.
- Optical equipment is expensive, which makes it hard to use for short-haul transmission (LANs, homes).
- Characteristics
    - Immune to electromagnetic interference.
    - Very low signal attenuation up to 100 km.
    - Very hard to tap (eavesdrop on).
- Usage: undersea links, long-haul telephone networks

<br/>

### Terrestrial Radio Channels

- Carry signals in the electromagnetic spectrum.
- Characteristics
    - Require no physical wire to be installed.
    - Can penetrate walls.
    - Provide connectivity to mobile users and can carry a signal over long distances.
    - Heavily affected by the propagation environment and the distance the signal travels.
        - Environmental factors
            - `Path loss`
            - `Shadow fading`: the signal strength decreases as it travels over distance / around or through obstructing objects
            - `Multipath fading`: caused by signal reflection off interfering objects
            - `Interference`: caused by other radio channels or electromagnetic signals
- Broadly classified into three groups
    - Channels that operate over very short distances of 1 to 2 m (wireless headsets, keyboards, etc.)
    - Local-area radio channels: channels that operate over ten to a few hundred meters (wireless LAN technology)
    - Wide-area radio channels: channels that operate over tens of kilometers (cellular access technology)

<br/>

### Satellite Radio Channels

- Link two or more Earth-based microwave transmitter/receivers known as `ground stations`.
- Process
    1. Receives the transmission on one frequency band
    2. Regenerates the signal using a `repeater`
    3. Transmits the signal on another frequency band
- Rate: gigabits per second
- Two kinds
    - `Geostationary satellites`: launched to 36,000 km above the Earth and permanently stay in one spot
    - `Low-earth orbiting (LEO) satellites`: orbit the Earth and can communicate with each other as well as with ground stations  
      _→ Might they be used for Internet access in the future?_
