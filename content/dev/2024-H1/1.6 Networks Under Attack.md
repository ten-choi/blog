# 1.6 Networks Under Attack

Behind all the usefulness and excitement of the Internet lies a dark side,  
where <b>"bad guys"</b> try to wreak havoc in our daily lives by damaging our Internet-connected computers, violating our privacy,  
and rendering inoperable the Internet services we depend on.

The `field of network security` is about how the bad guys can attack computer networks,  
how we can defend networks against those attacks, or better yet, how to design new architectures that are immune to such attacks in the first place.

<br/><br/>

## The bad guys can put malware into your host via the Internet

We attach devices to the Internet because we want to receive and send data.

Unfortunately, along with all the good stuff comes malicious stuff, collectively known as `malware`.  
Once malware gets into our devices, it can do all kinds of harm.

e.g., deleting files, or installing `spyware` that collects private information such as social security numbers, passwords, and keystrokes  
→ and then sends that information back to the bad guys over the Internet.

<br/>

A compromised host may also be enrolled in a network of thousands of similarly compromised devices, known as a `botnet`.  
The bad guys control and leverage these botnets for spam email distribution or `distributed DoS (Denial of Service) attacks` against targeted hosts.

<br/>

Much of the malware out there today is `self-replicating`.  
Self-replicating malware can spread exponentially in the following way:

1. Once it infects one host, **it seeks entry into other hosts over the Internet from that host.**
2. From the newly infected hosts, it seeks entry into yet more hosts.

<br/><br/>

## The bad guys can attack servers and network infrastructure

### DoS (Denial-of-Service) attacks

> Rendering a network, host, or other piece of infrastructure **unusable by legitimate users**

Web servers, email servers, DNS servers, and institutional networks can all be subject to DoS attacks.

<br/>

### Three categories of DoS attacks

#### Vulnerability attack

If the right sequence of packets is sent to a vulnerable application or operating system (including a few well-crafted messages),  
the service can stop or, worse, the host can crash.

<br/>

#### Bandwidth flooding

The attacker **sends a deluge of packets** to the target host so that its access link becomes clogged, preventing legitimate packets from reaching the server.

<br/>

*Recall the delay and loss analysis discussion from Section 1.4.2.*

If the server has an `access rate of R bps`, the attacker needs to send traffic at a rate of roughly `R bps` to cause damage.

<br/>

If R is very large, a single attack source may not be able to generate enough traffic to harm the server.

Furthermore, if all the traffic emanates from a single source, an upstream router may be able to detect the attack  
and block all traffic from that source before it gets near the server.

<br/>

In a `distributed DoS (DDoS) attack`, shown in the figure below, the attacker controls multiple sources and has each source blast traffic at the target.

<p align="center"><img width="550" alt="Distributed DoS attack" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210257676-334e4e5b-c06b-4b0b-9043-0fe4c2fab7d4.png">

With this approach, once the aggregate traffic rate across all the controlled sources reaches about `R`, the service can be crippled.

**DDoS attacks leveraging botnets with thousands of hosts** are very common today.

<br/>

#### Connection flooding

The attacker establishes a large number of **half-open or fully open TCP connections** at the target host.

The host becomes so bogged down with these bogus connections that it stops accepting legitimate ones.

<br/>

> What can computer network designers do to defend against DoS attacks?

→ Each of the three types of DoS attacks requires a different defense.

<br/><br/>

## The bad guys can sniff packets

Ubiquitous Internet access is extremely convenient and enables great new applications for mobile users, but it has also created a major security vulnerability.

By placing a passive receiver, a `packet sniffer`, near a wireless transmitter, that receiver can obtain **a copy of every packet being transmitted.**

Sniffers can be deployed in wired environments as well, not just wireless ones.  
(Ethernet LANs, cable access technologies, an institution's access router or access link to the Internet, and so on)

The bad guys can then analyze these captured packets offline to obtain **all kinds of sensitive information**, such as passwords, social security numbers, and trade secrets.

<br/>

Because packet sniffers are passive, that is, **they don't inject packets into the channel, they are difficult to detect.**

So when we send packets over a wireless channel, we have to accept the possibility that some bad guy may be recording copies of them.

<br/>

The best defense against packet sniffing involves cryptography. *(Covered in Chapter 8)*

<br/><br/>

## The bad guys can masquerade as someone you trust

It's surprisingly easy to create a packet with an arbitrary source address, packet content, and destination address, and then send it out onto the Internet.

**The ability to inject packets into the Internet with a false source address** is known as `IP spoofing`,  
and it's just one of many ways a user can pretend to be another user.

<br/>

Solving this problem requires `end-point authentication`,  
that is, **a mechanism that lets us be sure a message really originates from where we think it does.**

*How can this be done for network applications and protocols? (Covered in Chapter 8)*

<br/>

So how did the Internet end up facing these security problems in the first place?

<br/>

> The Internet was originally designed around the model of **"a group of mutually trusting users attached to a transparent network,"**  
> that is, **a model in which there's no need for security.** [Blumenthal 2001]


As a result, many aspects of the original Internet architecture reflect this notion of mutual trust.  
e.g., by default a user can send a packet to any other user, and a user's declared identity is taken at face value by default rather than being authenticated.

<br/>

But today's Internet clearly does not consist of "mutually trusting users."

Even so, today's users still need to communicate when they don't necessarily trust each other, when they want to communicate anonymously,  
or when they communicate indirectly through third parties (e.g., mobility-assisting agents).

<br/>

_Many security-related challenges still lie ahead of us._

We need to find ways to defend against sniffing, end-point masquerading, man-in-the-middle attacks, DDoS attacks, malware, and more.

> 💡 **Keep in mind that communication among mutually trusting users is the exception rather than the rule.**
