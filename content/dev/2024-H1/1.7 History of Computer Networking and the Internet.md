# 1.7 History of Computer Networking and the Internet

## 1.7.1 The Development of Packet Switching: 1961–1972

In the early 1960s, the world's dominant communication network was the **telephone network**.

The telephone network used `circuit switching` to transmit information from a sender to a receiver. (An appropriate choice, given that voice is transmitted at a constant rate between sender and receiver.)

<br/>

The traffic generated by each user, however, was `bursty`.

That is, **periods of activity (sending a command to a remote computer) alternated irregularly with periods of inactivity (waiting for a reply and reviewing it).**

<br/>

Three research groups around the world independently invented the notion of `packet switching` as an efficient and robust alternative to circuit switching.

- In 1967, Lawrence Roberts published an overall plan for the **ARPAnet**.  
  **→ The first packet-switched computer network and a direct ancestor of today's public Internet**
- In 1969, the first packet switch was installed at UCLA.
- By 1972, ARPAnet had grown to about 15 nodes.  
  The first host-to-host protocol between ARPAnet end systems, the <b>NCP (Network-Control Protocol)</b>, was completed. [RFC 001]

<br/>

With an end-to-end protocol available, applications could now be written.

- In 1972, Ray Tomlinson wrote the first email program.

<br/><br/>

## 1.7.2 Proprietary Networks and Internetworking: 1972–1980

The early ARPAnet was a `single, closed network`.

**In other words, to communicate with an ARPAnet host, you had to be actually attached to another ARPAnet IMP.**

<br/>

In the mid-1970s, other packet-switching networks separate from ARPAnet came into being.

- DARPA's packet-satellite network [RFC 829]
- Packet radio networks [Kahn 1978]
- **ALOHAnet**: a microwave network linking universities on the Hawaiian islands [Abramson 1970]
- Cyclades: a French packet-switching network led by Louis Pouzin [Think 2012]
- Time-sharing networks: networks from the late 1960s and early 1970s [Schwartz 1977]  
  e.g., Tymnet, the GE Information Services network

<br/>

As the number of networks grew,  
the time was ripe for developing <b>an encompassing architecture for connecting networks together (internetworking, a network of networks)</b>.

<br/>

These architectural principles were embodied in the `TCP protocol`.

It combined the following two functions and was quite different from today's TCP:

- **Reliable, in-sequence delivery of data** via end-system retransmission (still part of TCP today)
- **Forwarding functions** (performed by IP today)

<br/>

Early experimentation with TCP, combined with the recognition of **the importance of an unreliable, non-flow-controlled, end-to-end transport service** for applications such as packetized voice,  
led to **separating IP out of TCP** and **developing the UDP protocol**.

> The key Internet protocols we see today, TCP, UDP, and IP, were conceptually in place by the end of the 1970s.

<br/>

The **ALOHA protocol** [Abramson 1970] was `the first multiple access protocol`,  
allowing geographically distributed users to share a single broadcast communication medium (a radio frequency).

Abramson's work on multiple access protocols was built upon by Metcalfe and Boggs  
in developing the Ethernet protocol [Metcalfe 1976] for wire-based shared broadcast networks.

**In other words, some 25 years ago, well before the PC revolution and the explosion of networks, they were laying the groundwork for today's PC LANs.**

<br/><br/>

## 1.7.3 A Proliferation of Networks: 1980–1990

- By the end of the 1970s, about 200 hosts were connected to the ARPAnet.
- By the end of the 1980s, the number of hosts connected to the public Internet (a confederation of networks) had reached 100,000.

<br/>

The 1980s were thus a time of tremendous growth, driven largely by several efforts to create computer networks linking universities together.

- CSNET (Computer Science Network): created to link university researchers **who didn't have access to ARPAnet**.
- In 1986, <b>NSFNET (National Science Foundation Network)</b> was created to provide access to NSF-sponsored supercomputing centers.
- Starting with an initial backbone speed of 56 kbps, NSFNET's backbone was running at 1.5 Mbps by the end of the decade and served as the primary backbone linking regional networks.

<br/>

Many of the pieces of today's Internet architecture emerged from the ARPAnet community.

- On January 1, 1983, `TCP/IP` was officially deployed as the new standard host protocol for ARPAnet (replacing the NCP protocol).
- The transition from NCP to TCP/IP [RFC 801] was a `flag day` type event, meaning all hosts had to switch over to TCP/IP on the same day!

<br/>

In the late 1980s, important extensions were made to TCP to implement host-based congestion control. [Jacobson 1988]

- Development of the `domain name system (DNS)` [RFC 1034]
- It's used to **map domains** (human-readable Internet names, e.g., jw.edu, euna.com) **to 32-bit IP addresses**.

<br/>

In the early 1980s, France launched the Minitel project, an ambitious effort **to bring data networking into everyone's home**.

- A `public packet-switched network` (based on the X.25 protocol stack, which uses **virtual circuits**)
- It consisted of Minitel servers and inexpensive terminals with built-in low-speed modems.
- In 1984, the French government gave away a free Minitel terminal to every household that wanted one, and it became a huge success.
- Minitel sites included free sites, such as a telephone directory site, as well as private sites that charged users based on usage.

<br/><br/>

## 1.7.4 The Internet Explosion: The 1990s

The 1990s were marked by two events that symbolized the continued evolution and commercialization of the Internet.

1. ARPAnet, the progenitor of the Internet, ceased to exist.
   (In 1991, NSFNET lifted its restrictions on commercial use.)
2. The `World Wide Web (WWW)` emerged.

<br/>

The Web became the platform for enabling and deploying hundreds of new applications, including search, Internet commerce, and social networks.

- It was first created at CERN by Tim Berners-Lee between 1989 and 1991. [Berners-Lee 1989]
- He developed initial versions of the Web's four key components: HTML, HTTP, a Web server, and a browser.
- It built on ideas from early work on `hypertext` by Vannevar Bush in the 1940s  
  and by Ted Nelson from the 1960s onward.
- By the end of 1993, there were about 200 Web servers in operation.

<br/>

Around this time, several researchers were developing Web browsers with GUI interfaces,  
and companies large and small were running Web servers and starting to do business over the Web.

<br/>

The second half of the 1990s was a period of tremendous growth and innovation for the Internet.

By the end of the millennium, the Internet was supporting hundreds of popular applications, including the following four killer applications:

- Email, including attachments and Web-accessible email
- The Web, including Web browsing and Internet commerce
- Instant messaging, with contact lists
- Peer-to-peer file sharing of MP3s, pioneered by Napster

<br/><br/>

## 1.7.5 The New Millennium

In the first two decades of the 21st century, no technology has transformed society more than the Internet, along with Internet-connected smartphones.

And innovation in computer networking continues at a rapid pace.

<br/>

That said, the following developments deserve special attention.

<br/>

- We've witnessed an aggressive deployment of `broadband Internet access` to homes.  
  (Not only cable modems and DSL, but now 5G wireless service as well)

<br/>

- The rapid spread of `high-speed wireless Internet access`
    - This not only allows constant connectivity, but has also enabled new location-based applications  
      such as Yelp, Tinder, and Waze.
    - The number of wireless devices connected to the Internet surpassed the number of wired devices in 2011.

<br/>

- `Online social networks` such as Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter have created massive networks of people on top of the Internet.
    - Through their APIs, online social networks create platforms for new networked applications, including mobile payments, and for distributed games.

<br/>

- As discussed in Section 1.3.3, online service providers such as Google and Microsoft have built their own extensive `private networks`.
    - These not only connect their globally distributed data centers,  
      **but are also used to bypass as much of the Internet as possible by peering directly with lower-tier ISPs.**
    - As a result, Google delivers search results and email access almost instantaneously, as if its data centers were running inside the user's own computer.

<br/>

- `Cloud` companies not only provide applications with scalable computing and storage environments, but also give them access to high-performance private networks.
    - Many Internet commerce companies now run their applications "in the cloud" (Amazon's EC2, Microsoft's Azure, Alibaba Cloud).
    - Many companies and universities have also moved their Internet applications (e.g., email and Web hosting) to the cloud.
