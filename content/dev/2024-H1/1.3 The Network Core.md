# 1.3 The Network Core

Let's look at the mesh of packet switches and links that interconnects the end systems from Section 1.2.

The thick lines in the figure below represent the network core.

<p align="center"><img width="450" alt="The network core" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210137238-bf8d10d3-ef09-4263-9339-7fedbb2d619e.png">

<br/>
<br/>

There are two fundamental approaches to **moving data through a network of links and switches**:

1. `Packet switching`: not guaranteed (e.g., the Internet)
2. `Circuit switching`: resources are reserved → guaranteed

<br/>
<br/>
<br/>

# 1.3.1 Packet Switching

End systems exchange `messages` with each other. (A message is sent **from a source end system to a destination end system**.)

<br/>

1. The sending system breaks the message into smaller chunks of data called `packets`.


2. Each packet travels through `communication links` and `packet switches`.
    - There are two types of packet switches: <b>routers</b> and <b>link-layer switches</b>.


3. Packets are transmitted over each communication link **at a rate equal to the full transmission rate of the link**.
    - If a source end system or packet switch sends a packet of `L bits` over a link with a rate of `R bps (bits per second)`,  
      the time to transmit the packet is `L/R seconds`.

<br/>
<br/>

## Store-and-Forward Transmission

> 💡 The switch must receive the **entire packet** before it can begin to transmit the first bit of the packet onto the outbound link.

`Store-and-forward transmission` is used by most packet switches.

<br/>

<p align="center"><img width="500" alt="Store-and-forward packet switching" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210137289-63c6e459-0892-4ee6-8a91-d35fe4f9fb51.png">

<br/>
<br/>

Above is a very simple network made of two end systems connected by a single router.

- The source has three packets (1, 2, 3) to send to the destination, and **each packet consists of L bits.**
- The source **transmits L-bit packets over the link at R bps (bits per second)**.

<br/>

As the figure shows, suppose the source has transmitted `part of packet 1`, and that part has already arrived at the router.

Because the router uses store-and-forward, it can't transmit the bits it has received yet. Instead, the following happens:

1. It first `buffers (i.e., "stores")` the packet's bits.
2. Only **once the router has received all of the packet's bits** does it begin to `transmit (i.e., "forward")` the packet onto the outbound link.

<br/>

### Calculating the Elapsed Time

```
1-1. Let's calculate the time elapsed from when the source starts sending packet 1 until the destination has received all of packet 1.
```

*Here we ignore propagation delay, i.e., the time it takes for bits to travel across the wire at near the speed of light.  
→ Discussed in Section 1.4*

- `0 seconds`: the source starts transmitting packet 1
- `L/R seconds`
    - The source has finished transmitting all of packet 1, and the whole packet has been received and stored at the router. (There's no propagation delay.)
    - **Because the router now has the entire packet**, it begins transmitting it onto the outbound link toward the destination.
- `2L/R seconds`: the router has transmitted the entire packet, and the destination has received all of packet 1. (There's no propagation delay.)

<br/>

So with store-and-forward transmission the total delay is `2L/R`,  
whereas if a switch forwarded bits as soon as they arrived, without store-and-forward, the total delay would be `L/R`.

But routers need to receive, store, and process the entire packet before forwarding it. *(This is also discussed in detail in Section 1.4.)*

<br/>

```
1-2. Let's calculate the total time elapsed from the moment the source starts sending packet 1 until the destination has received all three packets (1, 2, 3).
```

- `0 seconds`: the source starts transmitting packet 1
- `L/R seconds`
    - The router has received packet 1 and starts transmitting it
    - The source starts transmitting packet 2
- `2L/R seconds`
    - The router has received packet 2 and starts transmitting it
    - The destination has received all of packet 1
    - The source starts transmitting packet 3
- `3L/R seconds`
    - The router has received packet 3 and starts transmitting it
    - The destination has received all of packet 2
- `4L/R seconds`: the destination has received all of packet 3

<br/>

So with store-and-forward transmission, the destination receives all three packets at `4L/R` seconds.

<br/>

## Queuing Delay and Packet Loss

* Each packet switch has multiple `links` attached to it, and **for each attached link** the packet switch **has an** `output buffer`.
* The output buffer, also called the output queue, **stores packets that the router is about to send into that link.**  
  It plays a key role in `packet switching`.

The store-and-forward delay we saw above isn't the only delay a packet experiences.

<br/>

```
An arriving packet needs to be transmitted onto a link. But what if that link is busy transmitting another packet?
```

**→ The arriving packet has to wait in the output buffer.** = `queuing delay`

- Queuing delay is variable and depends on how congested the network is.
- Because buffer space is finite, `packet loss` can occur.
- That is, if the buffer is already full of other packets waiting for transmission, <b>either the arriving packet or one of the already-queued packets is dropped</b>.

<br/>

Take a look at the example below.

## Forwarding Tables and Routing Protocols

> A router takes a packet arriving on one of its attached communication links and **forwards that packet onto another one of its attached communication links.**
>

So how does the router decide which link to forward the packet onto?

_Packet forwarding is actually done in different ways in different types of computer networks. Here we briefly describe how it's done in the **Internet**._

<br/>

### IP Addresses

- Every end system on the Internet has an IP address, and these addresses have a hierarchical structure.
- When a source end system wants to send a packet to a destination end system, **the source includes the destination's IP address in the packet's header.**

<br/>

### Forwarding Table

Each router has a forwarding table that **maps destination addresses (or portions of them) to the router's outbound links**.

So the process by which a router decides which link to forward a received packet onto looks like this:

1. A packet arrives at the router.
2. The router examines the packet's IP address.
3. It looks up that destination address in its forwarding table.
4. It sends the packet out on the corresponding outbound link.

<br/>

### Routing Protocols

So how do forwarding tables get set? *(Discussed in detail in Chapter 5)*

The Internet has a number of special routing protocols that are **used to set forwarding tables automatically**.

e.g., determine the shortest path from each router to each destination → use those shortest-path results to configure the routers' forwarding tables.

<br/>
<br/>

# 1.3.2 Circuit Switching

- In a circuit-switched network, the <b>resources needed along a path (buffers, link transmission rate)</b> to provide communication between end systems  
  are `reserved` for the duration of the communication session. (↔︎ packet-switched networks)
- In packet-switched networks, by contrast, session messages use resources `on demand`.
- As a result, they **may have to wait in a queue** for access to a communication link.

<br/>

- Connection = `circuit`: the switches on the path between sender and receiver must maintain the connection state.

1. Before the sender can send any information, the network must establish a **connection** between the sender and the receiver.
2. When the network establishes the circuit, it **reserves a constant transmission rate in the network's links** for the duration of the connection.
3. Because that transmission rate is reserved for the sender-receiver connection, **the sender can transfer data to the receiver at a** `guaranteed` **constant rate.**

<br/>

### End-to-End Connection

The figure below shows a circuit-switched network made up of four switches and four links.

Each link has four circuits, so **each link can support four simultaneous connections.**

<p align="center"><img width="470" alt="Circuit-switched network" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210137402-59bd4469-e343-430c-96df-9f5c6c072005.png">

<br/>
<br/>


When two hosts want to communicate, the network establishes a dedicated `end-to-end connection` between them.

So for host A to communicate with host B, the network first **reserves one circuit on A's link and one circuit on B's link**.  
(In the figure above: the second circuit on link (0, 0) and the second circuit on link (1, 1).)

For the duration of the connection, the connection gets one quarter of each link's total transmission capacity. (Because each link has four circuits.)

<br/>

---

<br/>

```
By contrast, what happens when one host wants to send a packet to another host over a packet-switched network such as the Internet?
```

As with circuit switching, the packet is transmitted over a series of communication links.

Unlike circuit switching, however, **with packet switching the packet is sent into the network without reserving any link resources.**

<br/>

> 💡 A packet-switched network makes no guarantee that data will be delivered within a certain amount of time.

<br/>
<br/>

## Multiplexing in Circuit-Switched Networks

How a circuit in a link is implemented:

1. `Frequency-Division Multiplexing (FDM)`: each circuit **continuously** gets a **fraction** of the bandwidth.
2. `Time-Division Multiplexing (TDM)`: each circuit gets the **full** bandwidth <b>periodically</b> (for short intervals of time, i.e., during slots).

<br/>

### Frequency-Division Multiplexing (FDM)

- Connections established across a link share the frequency spectrum of that link.
- The link **dedicates a fixed frequency band** to each connection for the duration of the connection. = `bandwidth`

<p align="center"><img width="300" alt="FDM" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210137431-63860cea-2921-47f8-89f4-19656ee81de5.png">

<br/>
<br/>

### Time-Division Multiplexing (TDM)

- A TDM link **divides time into frames of fixed duration**, and each frame is divided into a fixed number of `time slots`.
- When the network establishes a connection across a link, it **dedicates one time slot in every frame to that connection**.
- Transmission rate: number of bits in a slot × frame rate

<p align="center"><img width="350" alt="TDM" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210137432-426fd263-6cac-45ab-8262-a82bf772e1cc.png">

<br/>

### Packet Switching vs. Circuit Switching

**[ Proponents of packet switching ]**

- Claims
    1. Packet switching shares transmission capacity more efficiently than circuit switching.
    2. Packet switching is simpler, more efficient, and cheaper to implement than circuit switching.
- Reasoning
    - With circuit switching, resources must always be **reserved** for **each** user in order to communicate.
    - During `silent periods`, the allocated circuit sits idle while still holding its resources, which lowers resource utilization.
    - In other words, packet switching is more efficient because in circuit switching unused network resources (frequency bands or slots on the links along the connection's path) **can't be used by other ongoing connections instead**.

<br/>

**[ Opponents of packet switching ]**

- Claim: packet switching isn't suitable for real-time services.
- Reasoning: **end-to-end delays**, caused mainly by queuing delays (irregular and unpredictable)

<br/>

Are the opponents right?

To find out, let's look at two simple examples.

<br/>

---

<br/>

```
1. Suppose users share a 1 Mbps link, and each user alternates between periods of activity and periods of inactivity.
A user is active only 10% of the time and idle the remaining 90% of the time.
```

- Active period: the user generates data at a constant rate of 100 kbps
- Inactive period: the user generates no data

<br/>

✅ With `circuit switching`, 100 kbps must be reserved for each user at all times.  
Using TDM circuit switching as an example, if a one-second frame is divided into 10 time slots of 100 ms each, each user is allocated **one time slot per frame**.  
So the circuit-switched link **can support only 10 (= 1 Mbps / 100 kbps) simultaneous users.**

<br/>

✅ With `packet switching`, the probability that a specific user is active is 10%.  
As long as there are 10 or fewer simultaneously active users (a probability of 99.96%), the aggregate arrival rate of data is less than or equal to 1 Mbps (the link's output rate).  
So **when there are more than 10 simultaneously active users, the aggregate arrival rate of packets exceeds the link's output capacity and the output queue starts to grow.**  
(The queue keeps growing until the aggregate input rate falls back below 1 Mbps, at which point the queue length starts to shrink.)

<br/>

Since the probability of having more than 10 simultaneously active users is tiny (0.04%),  
**packet switching almost always delivers the same delay performance as circuit switching** while allowing more than three times as many users.

<br/>

---

<br/>

```
2. Suppose there are 10 users. As in example 1, they share a 1 Mbps link.
One user suddenly generates 1,000 packets of 1,000 bits each, while the other users generate no packets.
```

<br/>

✅ Let's look at `circuit switching` first.  
Using TDM circuit switching as an example, if a frame consists of 10 slots and each slot holds 1,000 bits,  
the user **can use only one time slot per frame** to transmit data, while the other nine slots in each frame sit idle.  
So it **takes 10 seconds to transmit all one million bits** of the user's data.

<br/>

✅ With `packet switching`,  
since no other users are generating packets, no multiplexing is needed, and the user can keep sending packets at the full 1 Mbps link rate.  
So all one million bits of the user's data are **transmitted in just 1 second.**


<br/>

---

<br/>

As the two examples above clearly show, **packet switching outperforms circuit switching.**

That's why today's telecommunication networks are trending toward packet switching.

<br/>

The key difference between the two approaches to sharing a link's transmission rate can be summarized as follows:

- Circuit switching: pre-allocates use of the transmission link regardless of demand.
- Packet switching: allocates link use **only on demand**.

<br/>

# 1.3.3 A Network of Networks

### Access ISPs

- `ISP (Internet Service Provider)`: a network of packet switches and communication links
    1. Provides a variety of network access to end systems (e.g., residential broadband access, high-speed LAN access, mobile wireless access)
    2. Provides Internet access to content providers (CPs) → lets them connect websites and video servers directly to the Internet.


- End systems (PCs, smartphones, web servers, etc.) **connect to the Internet through an access ISP.**
- Access ISPs provide wired or wireless connectivity using a variety of access technologies (DSL, cable, FTTH, WiFi, cellular, etc.).

<br/>

An access ISP doesn't have to be a telco or a cable company.  
e.g., a university providing Internet access to its students, staff, and faculty, or a company providing Internet access to its employees

But connecting end users and content providers to access ISPs is only part of the story.

**The access ISPs themselves must be interconnected**, and that's how the `network of networks` was born.

<br/>

> 💡 Goal: interconnect the access ISPs **so that all end systems can send packets to each other**

The simplest approach would be to connect each access ISP directly to every other access ISP.

But such a mesh design would be far too costly for the access ISPs, since each one would have to maintain separate communication links to hundreds of thousands of other access ISPs around the world.

<br/>

To understand today's Internet structure, let's build up a series of network structures step by step.

<br/>

## Network Structure 1

> Interconnect all access ISPs with **a single global transit ISP**.

- `Global transit ISP`: a network of routers and communication links that spans the globe and has at least one router near each of the hundreds of thousands of access ISPs
- Building such an extensive network would be very expensive for the global ISP.
- To make a profit, the global ISP **charges each access ISP for connectivity**.
    - The access ISP is the `customer`
    - The global ISP is the `provider`

<br/>
<br/>

## Network Structure 2

If one company builds and operates a profitable global ISP, it's natural for other companies to build their own global ISPs and compete.

This leads to Network Structure 2.

<br/>

> Hundreds of thousands of access ISPs and **multiple global ISPs**

- `Two-tier hierarchy`
    - Top tier: the global transit providers
    - Bottom tier: the access ISPs
- **The global ISPs must be interconnected with each other.**
- Otherwise, access ISPs connected to one global ISP couldn't communicate with access ISPs connected to another global transit provider.

<br/>

### Regional ISPs and Tier-1 ISPs

In reality, no ISP has a presence in every city in the world.

Instead, in any given region there may be a `regional ISP` to which the access ISPs in that region connect, and each regional ISP connects to `tier-1 ISPs`.  
*(In practice, tier-1 ISPs don't have a presence in every city in the world either.)*

<br/>
<br/>

## Network Structure 3

> **Multi-tier hierarchy (access ISPs, regional ISPs, tier-1 ISPs)** *→ roughly resembles today's Internet*

There are several competing tier-1 ISPs, and there may be several competing regional ISPs in a region.

In more complex cases, there may be larger regional ISPs to which smaller regional ISPs connect.

<br/>

Billing in this hierarchy works roughly like this.

Because a customer ISP pays its provider ISP to obtain global Internet interconnectivity,  
you can say that "there's a customer-provider relationship at each level."

1. Each **access ISP** pays the **regional ISP** it connects to.
2. Each **regional ISP** pays the **tier-1 ISP** it connects to.
3. Tier-1 ISPs sit at the top of the hierarchy, so they don't pay anyone.

<br/>
<br/>

## Network Structure 4

> Multi-tier hierarchy (access ISPs, regional ISPs, tier-1 ISPs) + **PoPs + multi-homing + peering + IXPs**

<br/>

To build a network that more closely resembles today's Internet, we need to add the following four items to Network Structure 3.

- `PoP (Points of Presence)`
    - Simply a group of one or more routers (at the same location) in the provider's network
    - Exists at every level of the hierarchy except the bottom (access ISP) level, and is **where customer ISPs can connect to the provider ISP.**
    - To connect to a provider's PoP,  
      a customer **can lease a high-speed link from a third-party telecommunications provider** to directly connect one of its routers to a router at the PoP.


- `Multi-homing`
    - **Connecting to two or more provider ISPs**
    - e.g., an access ISP connecting to two regional ISPs, or to two regional ISPs and a tier-1 ISP
    - Any ISP except a tier-1 ISP can choose to multi-home.
    - When an ISP multi-homes, it can keep sending and receiving packets to and from the Internet even if one of its providers fails.


- `Peering`
    - To reduce what customer ISPs pay their provider ISPs, **nearby ISPs at the same level of the hierarchy can peer.**  
      (When two ISPs peer, it's typically settlement-free, meaning neither pays the other.)
    - In other words, they directly connect their networks so that all traffic between them **passes over the direct connection rather than through upstream ISPs**.
    - Tier-1 ISPs also peer with one another.


- `IXP (Internet Exchange Point)`
    - A meeting point where multiple ISPs can peer together
    - Usually housed in a stand-alone building with its own switches.

<br/>
<br/>

## Network Structure 5

> Multi-tier hierarchy (access ISPs, regional ISPs, tier-1 ISPs) + PoPs + multi-homing + peering + IXPs + **content-provider networks**

<br/>

This describes the Internet as of 2012, and **Google** is one example of a company driving these content-provider networks.

- All of Google's data centers are interconnected via Google's private TCP/IP network, which spans the globe and is separate from the public Internet.
- **Google's private network carries only traffic to and from Google servers.**
- That is, by peering with lower-tier ISPs (connecting directly with them or connecting with them at IXPs), it "`bypasses`" the upper tiers of the Internet.  
  (See the figure below.)

<br/>

- Because many access ISPs can still be reached only by transiting through tier-1 networks,  
  Google's network also connects to tier-1 ISPs and pays them for the traffic it exchanges with them.
- Benefits content providers get from building their own networks
    - They can reduce what they pay to upper-tier ISPs.
    - They gain more control over how their services are ultimately delivered to end users.

<br/>

To sum it all up:

> 💡 Today's <b>Internet (a network of networks)</b> is made up of a dozen or so tier-1 ISPs and hundreds of thousands of lower-tier ISPs.

- Lower-tier ISPs connect to upper-tier ISPs, and upper-tier ISPs interconnect with each other.
- Users and content providers are customers of lower-tier ISPs, and lower-tier ISPs are customers of upper-tier ISPs.
- Recently, major content providers have also built their own networks and connect directly to lower-tier ISPs wherever possible.
