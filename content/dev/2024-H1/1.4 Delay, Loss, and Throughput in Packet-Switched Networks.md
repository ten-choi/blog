# 1.4 Delay, Loss, and Throughput in Packet-Switched Networks

> The Internet is an **infrastructure that provides services to distributed applications** running on end systems. (Section 1.1)

Ideally, we'd like Internet services to move as much data as we want between any two end systems, instantaneously, without any loss of data.

In reality that's hard to achieve: computer networks **constrain throughput**, the amount of data per second that can be transferred between two end systems.

**This introduces delays between end systems and can also cause packets to be lost.**

<br/>
<br/>
<br/>

# 1.4.1 Overview of Delay in Packet-Switched Networks

A packet starts in one host (the source), passes through a series of routers, and ends its journey in another host (the destination).

As a packet travels from one node to the next along its path, it suffers several types of delay **at each node** along the way,  
and these add up to the `total nodal delay`.

- `Nodal processing delay`
- `Queuing delay`
- `Transmission delay`
- `Propagation delay`

<br/>

Let's get the big picture with the figure below, which shows the nodal delay at router A.

<p align="center"><img width="470" alt="Nodal delay" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210216460-d4e1eacc-cde9-4185-aca8-661e78a20427.png">

<br/>
<br/>


As part of its end-to-end route between source and destination, a packet is sent from an upstream node through router A to router B.

- `Upstream`: the flow of data sent from the client to the server
- `Downstream`: the flow of data sent from the server to the client. Downstream traffic is usually much larger than upstream traffic.

<br/>

Router A has `one outgoing link` leading to router B, and that link is preceded by a `queue (buffer)`.

1. The packet arrives at router A from the upstream node.
2. Router A examines the packet header to determine the appropriate outbound link for the packet.
3. Router A sends the packet onto the chosen link. (In the figure, the chosen link = the link leading to router B.)

<br/>

```
What happens if another packet is currently being transmitted on the link to router B, or if other packets are already waiting ahead of it in the queue?
```

The newly arriving packet joins the queue to wait for its turn on the link.

<br/>

_Several kinds of delay occur throughout this whole process._

<br/>

### Processing Delay

> 💡 The time required to examine the packet's header and determine where to direct the packet

- It can also include other factors, such as the time needed to **check for bit-level errors in the packet** that occurred while transmitting the packet's bits from the upstream node to router A.
- After this nodal processing, the router directs the packet to the queue that precedes the link to router B.
- Processing delays in high-speed routers are typically on the order of microseconds.

<br/>

### Queuing Delay

> 💡 The time a packet spends waiting in the queue to be transmitted onto the link

- The length of a specific packet's queuing delay **depends on the number of earlier-arriving packets** that are queued and waiting for transmission onto the link.
- Delay can vary significantly from packet to packet.
    - If the queue is empty and no other packet is being transmitted, the packet's queuing delay → 0
    - If traffic is heavy and many other packets are waiting to be transmitted, the packet's queuing delay → becomes very long.
- Microseconds to milliseconds

<br/>

### Transmission Delay

> 💡 The time required to push (that is, transmit) all of the packet's bits onto the link

Assume packets are transmitted **first-come-first-served (FIFO)**. (A packet is transmitted only after all the packets that arrived before it have been transmitted.)

Let the length of the packet be `L bits` and the transmission rate of the link from router A to router B be `R bps`. (R is determined by the transmission rate of the link to router B.)

Then the transmission delay is `L/R`. (Microseconds to milliseconds)

<br/>

### Propagation Delay

> 💡 The time required for a bit to propagate from the beginning of the link at router A to router B

- A bit propagates at the propagation speed of the link.
- The propagation speed depends on the link's physical medium (fiber optics, twisted-pair copper wire, etc.) and ranges from 2×(10^8) meters/sec to 3×(10^8) meters/sec.  
  → Equal to, or a little less than, the speed of light.

If the distance between routers A and B is `d` and the link's propagation speed is `s`, the propagation delay is `d/s`. (Typically a few milliseconds)

<br/>

### Comparing Transmission Delay and Propagation Delay

#### Transmission Delay

- The time it takes **for the router to push out the packet**
- A function of the packet's length and the link's transmission rate → has nothing to do with the distance between the two routers.

#### Propagation Delay

- The time it takes for a bit **to propagate from one router to the next**
- A function of the distance between the two routers → has nothing to do with the packet's length or the link's transmission rate.

<br/>
<br/>

## Total Nodal Delay

> 💡 Total nodal delay = processing delay + queuing delay + transmission delay + propagation delay

<br/>

The contribution of each delay component can vary significantly.

#### Propagation Delay

- On a link connecting two routers on the same campus, it's around 2 microseconds → negligible
- For two routers connected by a geostationary satellite link, it's hundreds of milliseconds → becomes the dominant term in the total nodal delay.

#### Transmission Delay

- For rates of 10 Mbps and higher, as with a LAN → negligible
- For Internet packets sent over low-speed dial-up modem links, it can reach hundreds of milliseconds.

#### Processing Delay

- Usually negligible in the total nodal delay.
- However, it strongly influences a router's maximum throughput, i.e., the maximum rate at which it can forward packets.

<br/>
<br/>
<br/>

# 1.4.2 Queuing Delay and Packet Loss

Let's take a closer look at the most interesting component of <b>nodal delay (the delay at a single router)</b>: queuing delay.

Unlike the other three delays, queuing delay can vary from packet to packet.

<br/>

e.g., what if 10 packets arrive at an empty queue at the same time?

- The first packet transmitted suffers no queuing delay.
- The last packet transmitted suffers a large queuing delay (it waits for the other nine packets to be transmitted).

That's why queuing delay is **generally characterized with statistical measures**, such as the average queuing delay, the variance of queuing delay, and the probability that the queuing delay exceeds some specified value.

<br/>

### Main Factors That Determine Queuing Delay

- The rate at which traffic arrives at the queue
- The transmission rate of the link
- The nature of the arriving traffic (does traffic arrive periodically or in bursts?)

<br/>

---

<br/>

Suppose the following:

```
Average rate at which packets arrive at the queue: a packets/sec
Transmission rate (the rate at which packets are pushed out of the queue): R bits/sec
All packets are L bits
The queue is so large that it can hold an infinite number of bits
```

Then the `traffic intensity` (**the average rate at which bits arrive at the queue divided by the link's transmission rate**) is `La/R`.  
(Traffic intensity is very important for estimating the extent of queuing delay.)

<br/>

✅ If `La/R > 1`, **the average rate at which bits arrive at the queue exceeds the rate at which bits can be transmitted from the queue**.

So the queue grows without bound and the queuing delay approaches infinity. *→ Design your system so that the traffic intensity is no greater than 1.*

<br/>

✅ If `La/R ≤ 1`, the nature of the arriving traffic affects the queuing delay.

- If one packet arrives periodically every L/R seconds, every packet arrives at an empty queue → there's no queuing delay.
- If packets arrive in bursts, there can be a significant **average queuing delay**.

<br/>

---

<br/>

In general, packet arrivals don't follow a fixed pattern; they're spaced apart by random amounts of time.

The graph below shows **the qualitative dependence of average queuing delay on traffic intensity**.

<p align="center"><img width="320" alt="Dependence of average queuing delay on traffic intensity" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210216463-79992219-167f-4340-ab30-31383deac4d3.png">

<br/>
<br/>

> 💡 As the traffic intensity approaches 1, the average queuing delay increases rapidly.

- When the traffic intensity is close to 0
    - Packet arrivals are few and far between, so an arriving packet is unlikely to find another packet in the queue.
    - So the average queuing delay is close to 0.


- When the traffic intensity is close to 1
    - There will be intervals when the arrival rate exceeds the transmission capacity, and a queue will form.
    - The queue shrinks when the arrival rate drops below the transmission rate.

<br/>

### Packet Loss

Above we assumed the queue can hold an infinite number of packets, but in reality a queue has **finite capacity**, which depends heavily on the switch design and cost.

So as the traffic intensity approaches 1, the packet delay doesn't actually go to infinity; instead, the queue fills up.

<br/>

> 💡 When the queue is full and has no room to store a packet, the router **drops** that packet.

- **The fraction of lost packets increases as the traffic intensity increases.**
- Lost packets may be **retransmitted** end to end to ensure that all data is eventually delivered to the destination.

<br/>

---

<br/>

To sum up, **performance at a node** is measured by two things:

- Delay
- Probability of packet loss

<br/>
<br/>
<br/>

# 1.4.3 End-to-End Delay

To look at **the delay from source to destination**, consider the following situation.

```
There are N-1 routers between the source host and the destination host.
The network is uncongested, so queuing delays are negligible.
The transmission rate out of each router and out of the source host is R bits/sec.
The packet size is L bits.
```

> End-to-end delay = N(processing delay + transmission delay (L/R) + propagation delay)

<br/>

*This generalizes the end-to-end delay formula from Section 1.3, which didn't account for processing and propagation delays.*

# 1.4.4 Throughput in Computer Networks

There are three key performance measures in computer networks:

- Delay
- Probability of packet loss
- `End-to-end throughput`

<br/>

Consider transferring a large file from host A to host B across a computer network.

If the file consists of **F bits** and it takes **T seconds** for host B to receive all F bits,

- The `instantaneous throughput` at any instant = the rate (bits/sec) at which host B is receiving the file
- The `average throughput` = F/T bits/sec

<br/>

For applications like Internet telephony, it's desirable to have **low delay and an instantaneous throughput consistently** above some threshold.

For other applications, including file transfers, delay isn't critical, but it's desirable to have **the highest possible throughput**.

<br/>

---

<br/>

Let's look at two examples to think about throughput for a file transfer from a server to a client.

<p align="center"><img width="500" alt="File transfer throughput" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210216470-eff94fd0-1b04-4efd-aa60-85f7bad15507.png">

<br/>
<br/>

```
Figure (a) shows a server and a client connected by two communication links and a router.
(Assume the only bits being sent through the entire network are those from the server to the client.)

Rs: rate of the link between the server and the router
Rc: rate of the link between the router and the client
```

What's the **server-to-client throughput** here? *(Think of the bits as fluid and the communication links as pipes.)*

<br/>

The server can't pump bits through its link faster than Rs bps, and the router can't forward bits faster than Rc bps.

- If `Rs < Rc`: Rs bps
- If `Rc < Rs`: Rc bps
    - The router can't forward bits as quickly as it receives them.
    - The bits at the router waiting for transmission to the client will keep piling up.

> Throughput = min{Rc, Rs} = the transmission rate of the bottleneck link

<br/>
<br/>

```
Figure (b) shows a network with N links between the server and the client.
Transmission rates of the N links: R1, R2, ... , RN
```

The server-to-client throughput works the same way as in figure (a).

> Throughput = min{R1, R2, … , RN} = **the transmission rate of the bottleneck link** along the path between server and client

<br/>

---

<br/>

Here are two more examples that reflect today's Internet.

<p align="center"><img width="400" alt="End-to-end throughput" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/86337233/210216466-21232719-49ba-41e4-a6f9-af5491b6ffd2.png">

<br/>
<br/>

```
Figure (a) shows two end systems connected through a computer network.
(Assume the only bits being sent through the entire network are those from the server to the client.)

Rs: rate of the access link connecting the server to the network
Rc: rate of the access link connecting the client to the network

All the links in the core of the communication network have transmission rates much higher than Rs and Rc.
(That's actually the case: today's Internet core sees little congestion.)
```

Here too, the rate at which bits can flow from source to destination equals the minimum of Rs and Rc.

> Throughput = min{Rc, Rs}

<br/>

> 💡 In today's Internet, the constraining factor for throughput is typically the access network.

<br/>
<br/>

```
Figure (b) shows 10 servers and 10 clients connected to the core of a computer network,
with 10 simultaneous downloads taking place (10 client-server pairs).
(Assume these 10 downloads are the only traffic in the network at the moment.)

There is one link R in the core that all 10 downloads pass through.

R: transmission rate of link R
Rs: rate of every server access link
Rc: rate of every client access link

Assume the transmission rates of all links in the core (except the one common link of rate R) are larger than Rs, Rc, and R.
```

What's the **throughput of each download** here?

If the common link R is fast enough, the throughput of each download will be min{Rs, Rc}.

<br/>

**But what if the rate of the common link R is on the same order as Rs and Rc?**

e.g., Rs = 2 Mbps, Rc = 1 Mbps, R = 5 Mbps  
→ The shared link gives each download a throughput of 500 kbps, so the end-to-end throughput of each download drops to 500 kbps.

**In other words, the shared link in the core becomes the bottleneck for each download.**

<br/>

---

<br/>

To sum up the examples above in one line:

> 💡 Throughput depends not only on **the transmission rates of the links the data flows over**, but also on **interfering traffic**.
