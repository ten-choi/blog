I have two friends who are working programmers without a CS degree,

and plenty of acquaintances who prepared for IT jobs without one, so this is my subjective take based on them.

My own major was game development. I didn't have any big reason for choosing that path, though;

I just found along the way that server work suited my personality, so I switched.

Early on I did both frontend and backend, but now I've been working on the backend for a while.

These were the most common patterns:

1.  Going to a government-funded web development bootcamp to land an IT job
2.  Wanting to get into IT on their own and self-studying, even though it has nothing to do with their major
3.  Starting at their company's suggestion, based on a bit of prior experience

And the gap is big.

The difference in fundamentals — git, Linux, and so on — is pretty significant.

They probably spend less on learning, and they don't have a framework for how development should be done.

2\. Understanding algorithms and data structures

  To put it simply, there's a big difference even in how variables are named or in basic algorithms.

  For example, some people declare a variable as int a=0; while others write int result = 0;

  and now and then I see people paste the same code five times instead of using a loop.

3\. Weak search skills

  Setting up your first programming environment or dealing with an error usually comes down to searching.

  A lot of people struggle because they don't know how to go about solving the problem.

4\. Seeing the big picture

   I realized this is the most important one while teaching a friend of mine (who studies really hard).

   Of course I've put a lot of time into coding, but from where I stood, my friend was banging his head against a wall.

   There are gaps in knowing what kind of programmer you want to be, what language you're using, or how

   the code you write actually flows. It may be too early to worry about things like git or Linux, but

   knowing the concepts versus not knowing them makes a big difference.

### \- The Essentials

The title is about succeeding as a programmer, so your competition is people with CS degrees.

If you're just starting out, what matters is whether you can give up almost everything except eating, sleeping, and studying for at least 6 months to a year.

If you're aiming high, you need effort to match.

(My government-funded course was Japanese + web, and I did nothing but eat and study for about 7 months.)

**\- If you have some financial room**

For the basics, just put your money into bootcamps and video courses, no question.

Having a tutor who can get you unstuck right away is the icing on the cake.

The reason you need a tutor (an IT mentor) is clear: someone who helps with the parts that are hard to understand alone or where you're stuck,

and who broadens your perspective, is a huge help.

**\- If you don't have financial room**

A government-funded course is ideal. Look into whatever benefits the government offers job seekers,

squeeze as much as you can out of the instructors there, and back it up with YouTube or Inflearn.

Once you're employed, search skills matter a lot, so I'd recommend solving problems by searching whenever you can,

but when you're truly stuck, it's also important to grab the instructor after class, or make a strong classmate

your helper, so you can get help and push through quickly.

I usually study with a friend at a café, and in his case, I pointed out quite a few of the hard parts and key points he couldn't get through alone,

and each time I saw him make rapid progress. (He does work hard, too.)

What matters is putting in the effort yourself and studying consistently. Blogging like I do is a good option too.

Your number-one goal is growth and success in IT, but an offline bootcamp's number-one goal is your growth plus steady tuition income,

so don't get too tied down by it.

**\- Finally**

My English is really bad, but I started studying it at the end of March 2020.

English looks cool when you're good at it and it helps at work, so I've kept at it, and by sticking with it I can tell

I'm improving. IT seems similar. If you keep at it steadily and diligently, you'll get better.

Your growth speed can vary with your environment, but I've seen plenty of people who, once they reach the point where they can grow on their own,

just keep climbing from there.

Thanks for reading. Happy coding!
