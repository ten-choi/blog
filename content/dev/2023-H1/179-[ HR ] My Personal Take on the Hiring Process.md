In hiring, first impressions come down to two things:  
whether the candidate is late, and how they're dressed

They show you what attitude the candidate is bringing to your company  
Of course, everyone has a different bar  
For me, for a developer, jeans, ordinary clothes, and arriving about 2 minutes early is the bare minimum  
It's a field with a relaxed dress code where people are pretty calculated, so I think that's a reasonable line

If someone shows up in absurd clothes or late,  
I'd politely ask about it and see how they handle it  
After the greetings, most interviews start with some ice-breaking. That's how it went when I was interviewed too

How did you get here? Oh, you came a long way~  
What do you like? Do you play games, like LoL? You keep a light conversation going

There are 3 essential parts to an interview

1.  Technical questions
2.  Questions about projects
3.  A coding test

You move into those 3 naturally through the conversation  
An interviewer who actually wants to hire will read the resume and portfolio pretty carefully  
If there's a blog or a Git profile, they'll look at that too

Whether junior or experienced, if they got past the resume screen and made it to an interview,  
that means what they've done so far roughly matches what your company does  
Unless it's a tiny company that nobody applies to, in which case they'll just take anyone

Anyway: how well do they know what's in their portfolio?  
What tech did they use? What criteria did they use to choose it? You can do a bit of follow-up drilling

As the interviewer, I already have my answer in mind  
I'm asking, "I use this for this reason. Why did you use it?" Pretending to know what you don't is the worst  
And I don't think you can usually bluff your way to the right answer at an IT company  
Encryption, MSA, why you built a previous project the way you did, and so on  
You go through the basics like that

Once the technical part is done, you ask other things. Why did you leave your last company? Why did you apply here?  
The obvious reasons are salary or growth. I don't expect anything more dramatic than that  
That said, now and then someone is overly honest, and I've yet to see one of them get hired

Some companies also ask what you'll be doing a few years from now  
Whether I'm the interviewer or the candidate, I wish startups would skip questions like that  
Sure, I'd also like people at my company not to leave often  
but it feels clingy, like you're being pushed to say you'll still be doing something here years from now   
A rich company picking up a bright young person full of dreams makes sense. A startup with no money just needs people to do the work

Usually the first interview is "we want to get to know you," and the second is "let us tell you about us, what do you think?"  
That's the vibe to go for. Big companies and their equivalents need all sorts of vetting,  
but talent doesn't flock to startups like that

In the second interview, you have to tell them to join you. You passed the first-round bar, and we want you.  
So you go with "we'll tell you about our clear vision and the best parts of our culture"  
And if you felt in the first interview that this is someone you need? Say plenty of good things

Talent always slips away. If we felt someone was talented, they may already be holding offers from 3 other companies  
Also, the smaller the company, the more it tends to want people to do a bit of everything. Not many talented people want that  
Whether it's frontend, backend, or anything else, spell it out clearly in the job posting  
If you expect things you didn't announce, good people leave fast

Beyond that, once someone's hired, the only way to know is to work together. That's what a 3-month probation at 100% pay is for  
IT isn't a job where you just stare at a computer; collaboration is key  
You have to keep aligning on opinions, and that needs to happen sharply but amicably

For better or worse, team leads and team members evaluate each other.  
Working together, you learn their dev style, speed, accuracy, and so on: who this person is and whether they're worth working with  
If you're a team lead or in a leadership role, you need kindness and wit  
You stare at a computer for long hours, but in the end it's work done person to person  
You need to gently and properly learn how to work with each other  
The more you slip up here, the faster your people start quietly quitting

Job-hopping is the trend these days, especially in IT, and you need to read that trend   
You have to keep giving talent that's ready to leave the sense that working here beats switching jobs!

Everyone knows the fundamentals are money and benefits, but budgets are limited  
I think a company should at least be one its people can brag about to someone, on social media or wherever  
For anyone with a working life, people around them will ask what kind of company it is

and asking about benefits or salary is a pretty natural next step  
If your company is still small, you need to give people something they can brag about, even something modest  
After that, if someone is a person you can keep working with, you keep working together  
and if someone leaves, you just hire someone new
