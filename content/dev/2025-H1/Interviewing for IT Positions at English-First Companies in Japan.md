For every office job that involves a computer, the value of the emotional side of things — manners, the impression you make — and of the communication skills you need to work with people keeps going up.

I suspect the developer's role will lean more and more in that direction.
Technical skill obviously still matters, but the details feel like they're shifting.

It used to be that developers were defined by categories like Java, Golang, TypeScript, or C++. Now
more of the weight goes into organizing the domain, deciding how to apply caching, and how to handle security,
and which language to use is simply decided at the start based on what role the service plays.


If entry-level and repetitive work in every office job can be replaced by AI,
that means there's that much less reason to hire,
and while there are many kinds of competitive edge you can bring at that point, when it comes to development skills, the value of beginner-level or repetitive knowledge has clearly dropped.

So what abilities can I show in an interview?

1. Diligence: a blog or GitHub leaves a record
2. Communication: greeting people well at the start, keeping a bright face during the interview, and closing with a good goodbye
3. Explanation: similar to communication, but communication is about making a good impression, while this is about whether you reasoned your way to a conclusion based on evidence
4. Flexibility and receptiveness: how I take in what the other person says
5. Leadership: when explaining a project, it shows whether I drove it myself or just did what I was told
6. Technical skill: probably detailed questions about the technology itself
7. Foreign language ability: sometimes part of the interview itself is conducted in a foreign language

If those are the things being judged, there's a lot being evaluated, and technical skill is just one of them.
In the past, technology probably carried more weight. Of course the weight of technical skill depends on the position, but basically, if someone has worked in a field for 5 or 10 years and thinks logically, they can usually handle most tasks once they know what needs to be done —
unless it's something like robotics or AI development that requires a lot more study.


Many people say the junior developer is dead, and I agree.
And it's not just junior developers — it's fair to say juniors in every office job will soon be gone.

New-grad hiring will continue, of course, but it's also fair to say hiring for office jobs as a whole will shrink sharply.
