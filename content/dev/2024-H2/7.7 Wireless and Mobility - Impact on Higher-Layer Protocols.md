# 7.7 Wireless and Mobility: Impact on Higher-Layer Protocols

<br/>

In this chapter we saw that wireless and wired networks differ significantly at the `link layer (characteristics of the wireless channel such as fading, multipath, and hidden terminals)`  
and at the `network layer (the presence of mobile users whose point of attachment to the network changes dynamically)`.

So are there differences at the `transport layer` and `application layer` too?

<br/>
<br/>

## Transport-Layer Protocol: TCP

In one sense, you might be right to think TCP and UDP can simply be used in networks with wireless links.

But the `TCP transport protocol` in particular often performs very differently in wired and wireless networks, and the difference in performance is significant.

<br/>
<br/>

TCP `retransmits` segments that are lost or corrupted on the path between sender and receiver.

A mobile user can experience loss for the following reasons:

- Network congestion (router buffer overflow)
- Handoff (delays in rerouting segments to the mobile node's new point of attachment)

<br/>

In every case, the ACKs TCP sends from receiver to sender only indicate whether a segment was received successfully;  
when a segment is lost, the sender doesn't know why.

Whatever the reason, the sender retransmits the segment.

<br/>

TCP's `congestion control mechanism` likewise always responds by **shrinking the congestion window**, regardless of the cause.

But bit errors are far more common in wireless networks,  
and **when loss is caused by bit errors or a handoff, there's no need for the TCP sender to shrink its congestion window (i.e., cut its sending rate).**

In reality, packets may be getting through fine along the end-to-end path, with plenty of router buffer space and no congestion at all.

<br/>
<br/>

So in the early-to-mid 1990s, researchers realized that because of high bit error rates on wireless links and handoffs,  
**TCP's unconditional congestion-control response** could be a problem in wireless environments.

Three approaches can be used to deal with this.

<br/>

> ✅ Local recovery

Recover from bit errors **at the point where they occur (e.g., the wireless link)**.

Examples include the 802.11 ARQ protocol, or approaches that use both ARQ and FEC.

<br/>

The problem with this approach is that the TCP sender has no idea that its segments are traversing a wireless link.

<br/>

> ✅ TCP sender awareness of wireless links

One alternative to address the problem with local recovery is to **make the TCP sender and receiver aware of the wireless link**,  
so they can distinguish losses caused by congestion in the wired network from losses caused by errors or handoffs on the wireless link.

In other words, congestion control is applied only to losses caused by congestion in the wired network.

<br/>

> ✅ Split-connection approach

Split the wireless user's end-to-end connection into **two transport-layer connections** (one between the wireless user and the AP, the other between the AP and the destination endpoint).

The end-to-end connection is thus split into a wireless part and a wired part.

The transport protocol over the wireless segment can be a standard TCP connection or a recovery protocol designed to run on top of UDP.

<br/>

This approach is widely used in cellular networks and has delivered significant performance improvements.

<br/>
<br/>

## Application-Layer Protocols

Because the wireless spectrum is shared,  
**bandwidth is a very scarce resource** for applications running over wireless links (especially cellular wireless links).

Although wireless links pose hard problems for application-layer development,  
the mobility they provide has also made a wide range of location-aware and context-aware applications possible.

Wireless mobile networks will also play a central role in realizing the ubiquitous computing environments of the future.
